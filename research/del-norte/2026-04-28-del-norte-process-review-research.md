# Del Norte Process Review Research

**Issue:** 1501 W. Del Norte Street housing project  
**Research lane:** Post-approval process review  
**Status:** Active research / evidence-first  
**Purpose:** Evaluate how the Del Norte approval process was handled after the project moved through Planning Commission and City Council.

## Framing

This is not a stop-the-project file.

The project has already been approved. The purpose now is to review the process honestly and document where the City, applicant, and public process appear to have worked well, where the record is inconsistent, and where residents still deserve clearer answers.

The working question:

> Did the City and applicant handle the Del Norte process in a way that was clear, consistent, fair, and resident-centered?

This file should avoid unsupported corruption claims. The evidence should be sorted into confirmed facts, apparent inconsistencies, good practices, bad practices, unanswered questions, and follow-up opportunities.

## Confidence Labels

- 🟢 Confirmed: directly supported by official record.
- 🟡 Needs Follow-Up: supported in part, but unclear or incomplete.
- 🔴 Unverified: claim or concern not yet supported by records.

## What Appears to Have Been Handled Well

### 1. Outreach did occur

The record shows multiple outreach events before the Planning Commission approval process was complete. Meritage documented three meetings in 2025: July 8, August 13, and August 21.

Confidence: 🟢 Confirmed

### 2. The project was revised after resident concerns

The project did not remain frozen at the original 282-unit version. The record shows it changed to 260 units, then later to 232 units. The removal of the stacked-flat product between the 282-unit and 260-unit versions appears to be a meaningful project revision.

Confidence: 🟢 Confirmed for 282 to 260. 🟡 Needs full April cross-check for 260 to 232.

### 3. Environmental mitigation was not ignored on paper

The Mitigated Negative Declaration includes a Mitigation Monitoring and Reporting Program covering biological resources, cultural resources, paleontological resources, hazardous materials, noise, and tribal cultural resources.

Confidence: 🟢 Confirmed

### 4. Some resident issues became formal conditions

Conditions include garage-use language in CC&Rs, RV parking restrictions, construction-hour limits, graffiti-resistant coatings, wall/fencing plan review, enhanced side elevations, street work, sewer study requirements, and mitigation compliance obligations.

Confidence: 🟢 Confirmed

## Apparent Inconsistencies / Process Concerns

### 1. The project version changed multiple times, which can confuse residents

The record contains 282-unit, 260-unit, and 232-unit versions. This is not automatically improper, but it makes public understanding harder. If residents attended one meeting or read one notice, they may have been reacting to a version that later changed.

Confidence: 🟢 Confirmed

Follow-up question:

> At each stage, how clearly did the City explain what changed from the prior version?

### 2. Environmental and traffic analysis may not line up neatly with each later project version

Caltrans commented on the 282-unit version. The City response states the traffic analysis did not include freeway ramp queuing or a broader multi-modal conflict analysis because those elements were not required under City criteria or the results of the analysis.

This needs careful treatment. A smaller project can reduce impacts, but a later project revision does not automatically answer every earlier concern. The record should show whether each major revision was reviewed for consistency and whether the final 232-unit version had its own clear consistency analysis.

Confidence: 🟡 Needs Follow-Up

Follow-up question:

> Was the final 232-unit version supported by a clear environmental consistency finding, or were key analyses still tied to the 282-unit or 260-unit versions?

### 3. Caltrans raised safety and complete-streets concerns that may not have been fully adopted

Caltrans suggested freeway off-ramp queuing review, multi-modal conflict analysis, sidewalks, bike/ped improvements, traffic calming, curb extensions, and crosswalk improvements. The City response concluded the existing analysis was adequate under CEQA and City requirements.

This is not proof of bad faith. It is a strong process question: the City may have met its technical threshold while still leaving residents with practical safety and congestion concerns.

Confidence: 🟢 Confirmed that Caltrans raised concerns. 🟡 Needs Follow-Up on what was incorporated.

### 4. Outreach counts do not equal broad public understanding

The applicant documented outreach meetings and attendance counts, but attendance alone does not prove residents understood the changing project versions, final tradeoffs, traffic assumptions, or conditions of approval.

Confidence: 🟡 Needs Follow-Up

Follow-up question:

> Did the City provide a plain-English change summary showing 282 to 260 to 232, including what residents gained and lost?

## Good Faith Indicators

These are items that suggest the process was not simply rushed through without records or response:

- Multiple outreach meetings were documented.
- The project was revised down from the original version.
- The General Plan Amendment changed from a higher-density mix to Neighborhood Medium Residential only in the January version.
- CEQA comments were formally responded to.
- Mitigation monitoring requirements were included.
- Conditions of approval imposed several applicant/developer obligations.

## Resident-Centered Concerns

These are not accusations. They are areas where the process may have served the formal approval path better than the average resident.

- Project changes may have been hard for residents to track.
- Technical CEQA responses may not have answered everyday traffic concerns in plain English.
- Caltrans complete-streets suggestions appear broader than what the City required.
- Residents may not understand which concerns became enforceable conditions and which did not.
- The final public benefit package needs a plain-English explanation: park dedication, Little League field retention, fees, homebuyer assistance, and developer obligations.

## Follow-Up Questions

### Project Version Questions

- What exactly changed from 282 to 260?
- What exactly changed from 260 to 232?
- Did each version have a clear staff explanation?
- Was the final 232-unit version analyzed in a standalone consistency document?

### Outreach Questions

- How many total residents received notice at each stage?
- Was the notice radius expanded from 500 feet to 1,000 feet, and when?
- Were renters notified directly, or only property owners/occupants in the mailing area?
- Did outreach materials clearly explain project revisions?

### Traffic / Safety Questions

- Why were freeway ramp queuing and broader multi-modal conflict analysis not required?
- Which Caltrans suggestions were incorporated?
- Which Caltrans suggestions were rejected or not required?
- Were traffic calming measures between Del Norte, Nora, Sunset, Rowland, Monte Vista Elementary, transit stops, and the mall/civic center considered beyond CEQA minimums?

### Money / Public Benefit Questions

- How did park dedication and park in-lieu fee calculations change between project versions?
- How did Development Impact Fees change between project versions?
- What exactly does the homeownership assistance program require?
- Who verifies that the required program is created and used?
- What street rehabilitation costs or in-lieu payments will be required?

### Conditions / Enforcement Questions

- Who monitors mitigation compliance?
- How will construction noise and work-hour rules be enforced?
- Who enforces the garage-use requirement in CC&Rs?
- What happens if the HOA does not enforce parking rules?
- Which conditions must be satisfied before building permits, grading permits, demolition permits, final inspection, or certificate of occupancy?

## Working Conclusion

The records do not support a simple story of "nothing happened" or "the City ignored everyone." Outreach occurred, the project changed, environmental responses were prepared, and conditions were added.

The records also do not support closing the book. The process created real questions about clarity, version control, traffic review, resident understanding, and whether the final public benefits were explained in a way residents could judge for themselves.

The strongest StayJustIn posture is:

> Give credit where the record shows good process. Press hard where the record shows gaps, shifting versions, technical answers, or unanswered resident concerns.
